Most of the photographs on this site were taken with a phone. Not because a phone is better than a camera, but because it is the one I always have with me. This year I stopped treating that as a compromise and started testing it properly.

## Test before the launch

Before shooting at a phone launch event, I made a short video in mid-February to see how a phone handled night scenes and artificial light. The footage came out better than I expected, and it changed how I planned the shoot. If you know the limits of the tool, you stop fighting them on the night.

## Auto mode, mostly unedited

For two months I used the phone as most people would: auto mode, no editing. The point was to see what the camera does on its own, without a photographer hiding behind adjustments. The answer was that it is very good at the ordinary — people, streets, rooms, the light you actually live in.

The exception was light painting. Those need long exposures, so they were shot in expert mode. They are still unedited. The colour comes from moving a light source during the exposure, not from a slider afterwards.

## Light painting

Light painting is my favourite thing to do with a phone camera. A dark room, a long exposure, and a light you can move. The result is part portrait, part drawing. It is unpredictable in a way that keeps it interesting; you get one frame and find out afterwards whether it worked.

[See the light painting portraits](../../portfolio/projects/light-painting-portraits/)

## Flat-lays and small scenes

Flat-lays are the opposite discipline: everything is still, so every choice is deliberate. I entered a couple of photography challenges this year and won twice. The yellow theme in one of them came from a single object I liked and built the whole frame around.

[See the flat-lay challenge](../../portfolio/projects/nothing-flat-lay-photography-challenge/)

## What I look for

My favourite photo of the year is not the most technical one. It is a frame full of people, with a mood I could not have planned. The people, the vibe, the frame — when those line up, the camera barely matters.

That is the whole lesson. Learn the tool well enough that it disappears, then pay attention to what is in front of you.
